Earlier roots

The idea came
before the toolkit.

Siddhant traces the early seeds of StreamSense to his work on Room OS and his participation in the Digital Research Observatory. The ambition has grown into a toolkit for connecting human experience, physiological measurements and useful feedback.

His coauthored research spans a 2020 sensor-room concept for human-centred clinical data collection and a 2024 study of cognitive load and relaxation using wearable sensors.

03 / Analysis

ProSense

A Python framework for physiological preprocessing, feature extraction and experimental classification of load-induction versus yoga phases.

  • Brain (EEG), pulse (PPG/BVP), skin conductance (GSR), movement (ACC/GYRO) and temperature
  • Subject-independent evaluation and fusion
  • Windowed pulse scoring and recalibration tools

Signals worth
building on.

Combining brain, cardiac, skin-conductance and movement features helps distinguish cognitive-load and yoga phases in the study. ProSense reports 85.2% subject-independent accuracy across ten participants.

That result motivates the next step: exploring how physiological patterns could support more personal feedback on demand and recovery.

Work in progress & evidence notes
  1. Research origins. The Room OS and Digital Research Observatory connection reflects Siddhant's account of how the idea developed. The 2020 publication establishes coauthorship and the sensor-room concept, not direct code inheritance or sole ownership of those collaborative projects. It presents a concept and theoretical framework, not evidence of clinical effectiveness.
  2. Connected workflow. The three-layer diagram presents the research vision. Direct StreamSense-to-ProSense LSL integration is work in progress, rather than a delivered turnkey loop.
  3. Hardware and capture. Physical Muse, BITalino and Kinect verification, live LSL-to-skeleton-preview wiring and E4 fine-offset calibration remain work in progress. E4 is an offline import, not live capture. Capture roadmap ↗
  4. Evaluation scope. The 85.2% result is a later repository analysis, separate from the 2024 publication. It covers 10 participants, 28,700 aligned epochs and 199 features using five participant-grouped folds, median imputation and a class-balanced random forest. Epochs are repeated observations, not additional participants. No benchmark was independently reproduced for this page. Evaluation code ↗
  5. Pulse scoring. The repository reports approximately 37 ms per feature-window score in a held-out-participant replay. The raw pulse adapter uses nine BVP features and defaults to 60-second windows with 5-second steps. The 85.2% multimodal result does not apply to pulse-only input. Real-device validation and deployment-specific recalibration remain work in progress. Pulse adapter ↗
  6. Generalization. Study-phase classification is not general-purpose stress detection or a clinical diagnostic tool. Validation beyond the study protocol, including the envisioned morning-breathing use case, remains future work. Standalone PPG and temperature did not beat their majority baselines in the reported evaluation; PPG was 91.9% versus a 92.0% baseline.
  7. Study data and access. Raw recordings are identifying biometric data and are not public. The ProSense tree checked on November 5, 2026 does not include the study dataset, despite bundled-data wording in its README. Reproduction requires appropriate data access. Collaboration requests follow the corresponding-author route in the paper. Pavlovia experiment-source access requires an account. Data-access note ↗
